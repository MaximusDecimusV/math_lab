Chaos and Predictability

Every simulation on this page runs the same equation twice, from two starting points that differ by a tiny amount. Run A and run B obey identical, fully deterministic laws. In a chaotic system they still end up in completely different places, and how soon that happens is the limit of prediction.

One dimension: fixed points and bifurcations

In 1D, x′ = f(x) moves x along a line. Fixed points are zeros of f. A fixed point is stable when f′(x*) < 0, because nearby points flow back toward it. Nothing can oscillate here: a point on a line can only move monotonically, so 1D flows can never be chaotic.

A bifurcation is a qualitative change as a parameter crosses a threshold. Drag r and watch fixed points appear, collide or swap stability. In the harvested-fish model x′ = x(1 − x) − h, the population collapses at the saddle-node h = 1/4.

Left: f(x), with filled dots for stable fixed points, open dots for unstable ones, and arrows for the flow. Right: fixed points against r, solid where stable and dashed where unstable. The vertical line marks the current r.

Two dimensions: spirals, limit cycles and the Hopf bifurcation

In the plane, the Jacobian at a fixed point classifies it by trace τ and determinant Δ: a saddle if Δ < 0, a node or spiral if Δ > 0, stable if τ < 0. The system below is x″ + (x² − μ)x′ + x = 0. For μ < 0 the origin is a stable spiral. At μ = 0 a pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis, and for μ > 0 every orbit is drawn onto a limit cycle of radius about 2√μ. That is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation.

Two dimensions still cannot produce chaos. The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem says a bounded orbit in the plane must approach a fixed point or a closed orbit, because trajectories cannot cross. Chaos needs a third dimension.

Maps: period doubling and the Lyapunov exponent

Maps jump instead of flowing, so even one dimension is enough for chaos. In the logistic map xn+1 = r xn(1 − xn), the fixed point gives way to period 2 at r = 3, then 4, 8, 16 and so on. The doublings accumulate at r ≈ 3.5699, with the gaps shrinking by Feigenbaum's ratio δ ≈ 4.669. Beyond that point the Lyapunov exponent λ = lim (1/n) Σ ln|f′(xk)| is mostly positive. For the shift map 10x mod 1 it is exactly ln 10: each iteration reveals one more decimal digit of the starting point.

The double pendulum

Two rigid arms give a 4D phase space (two angles, two angular velocities). Energy is conserved, which leaves a 3D surface to move on, and that is enough room for chaos. At small amplitude the motion is nearly two coupled linear oscillators and stays regular. Released from high up, the twins agree for a few seconds and then do completely different things. The system is Hamiltonian, so it has no attractor. Its phase space is a chaotic sea with islands of regular motion.

Both arms start at the release angle, at rest, and run B's upper arm is offset by the gap. Try 0.3 rad, where the twins stay together, and then 2.0 rad.

The three-body problem

Each body is pulled by the other two: q̈i = Σ mj(qj − qi)/|qj − qi|³. Energy, momentum and angular momentum are conserved. With two bodies that is enough to solve the problem exactly, which gives Kepler's ellipses. With three it is not. Bruns and Poincaré proved there are no further integrals, so there is no general formula. There are special exact solutions. The figure-eight is stable, so nearby starts stay nearby. Lagrange's equilateral triangle with equal masses is unstable: it holds briefly and then breaks apart chaotically. In Burrau's Pythagorean problem, masses 3, 4 and 5 released from rest go through a series of close encounters.

Deterministic, but only predictable for so long

Suppose any predictor starts with an error δ₀. That error might come from measurement, from rounding, or from a neural network's one-step mistake. Chaos grows it like δ₀eλt, so it reaches a tolerance Δ at

tp ≈ (1/λ) · ln(Δ / δ₀)

The logarithm is the important part. Each tenfold improvement in accuracy buys only a fixed ln 10 / λ of extra time, about 2.5 time units for Lorenz.

How far did the neural networks see?

Three predictors were trained on thousands of exact simulations, then rolled out from initial conditions they had never seen:

  • step learns one time step and repeats it.
  • step + unroll is the same, fine-tuned on 8-step rollouts.
  • direct maps the initial condition to all 500 future states at once.

Breakdown is the median time until the error passes 10% of the system's size, measured in Lyapunov times. The exact twin is a perfect solver started with the step model's one-step error. No model with that accuracy could beat it.

λ = 0.906 for Lorenz, and λ = 0.52 for the three-body family (the median over the test starting conditions). Three-body training starts are Lagrange's triangle plus noise of std 0.03, with equal masses and G = 1.

Lorenz: the model is close to the limit. The step model's error grows at exactly the chaotic rate, about 15× above the exact twin, and it keeps working long after its 500-step training window. Mapping the initial condition straight to the future (direct) fails within one Lyapunov time.

Three bodies: the model, not chaos, is the limit. An exact solver with the same initial error stays within 10% for about 11 Lyapunov times. The learned step model lasts about 1. The measured reason is close encounters. When two bodies come within 0.1 of each other, the one-step error is 30× worse, and every encounter injects a kick that chaos then amplifies. Energy drifts 3% within 100 steps. Here the direct model wins, because it never feeds its own errors back through those encounters.

Changing the initial conditions. Larger perturbations shorten the three-body horizon steadily (105 → 75 → 57 steps for noise 0.03 → 0.06 → 0.10). A different family breaks every model within 10–30 steps: the figure-eight, Burrau's 3-4-5 masses, or Lorenz started off its attractor. A network only knows the part of phase space it was shown.
