Chapter 0

What is a dynamical system?

A dynamical system has two ingredients: a state, which is a list of numbers that fully describes the system right now, and a rule that says how the state changes. Give the rule a starting state (the initial condition) and the whole future is fixed.

For a continuous-time system the rule is a differential equation. The dot means "rate of change in time":

ẋ = f(x)

Two everyday examples:

  • Money with interest. ẋ = rx: the balance grows in proportion to itself, so x(t) = x₀ert.
  • Coffee cooling. Ṫ = −k(T − 20): the coffee heads toward room temperature, 20 °C, faster when it is hotter. Once T = 20 nothing changes. That is a fixed point.

Both are linear: f is a straight line in x. Linear systems can be solved with exponentials and sines, and doubling the initial condition just doubles the solution. Everything interesting in this course comes from nonlinear rules such as x², sin x or xy, where that superposition breaks. Most nonlinear systems have no formula for x(t) at all, so we learn to read their behaviour from the shape of f.

The number of state variables is the dimension. It turns out to be the most important single fact about a system: one dimension can't oscillate, two can't be chaotic, and three can.

Chapter 1

One dimension: fixed points and their stability

Think of ẋ = f(x) as a velocity field on a line. Where f > 0 the point moves right, where f < 0 it moves left, and where f(x*) = 0 it sits still. Plotting f against x gives the whole story without solving anything.

Take this example:

ẋ = x − x³ = x(1 − x)(1 + x)

Its fixed points are x* = −1, 0, 1. Between 0 and 1, f is positive, so points flow right toward 1. Just above 1, f is negative, so they flow back left. The point 1 attracts from both sides: it is stable. The origin repels: it is unstable.

The slope test, and the first appearance of λ

Zoom in on a fixed point. Write x = x* + η for a small deviation η. A Taylor expansion gives η̇ ≈ f′(x*) η, so

η(t) ≈ η₀ ef′(x*) t
  • f′(x*) < 0: small deviations shrink exponentially. Stable.
  • f′(x*) > 0: small deviations grow exponentially. Unstable.
  • f′(x*) = 0: the test says nothing, and the next term in the Taylor series decides.

For x − x³, f′(x) = 1 − 3x². At 0 it is +1 (unstable). At ±1 it is −2, so a nudge decays like e−2t.

Keep this in mind. The rate f′(x*) is the exponential rate at which nearby states separate or converge. Chapter 8 generalizes exactly this number to a moving trajectory, and calls it the Lyapunov exponent λ. Chaos is what you get when that rate stays positive forever, with no fixed point in sight.

Why a line can't oscillate

On a line the velocity at each point is a single number, so a trajectory can only move steadily one way until it reaches a fixed point or runs off to infinity. To come back it would have to pass a point where f changes sign, and that point is a fixed point it can never cross. No oscillation is possible, let alone chaos.

Chapter 2

Bifurcations: when a parameter changes the picture

Real systems have knobs: a growth rate, a fishing quota, a temperature difference. Usually turning a knob just shifts the fixed points a little. At special values, called bifurcation points, fixed points appear, vanish or swap stability, and the behaviour changes qualitatively. There are three basic kinds in 1D:

TypeNormal formWhat happens as r increases through 0
Saddle-nodeẋ = r + x²Two fixed points (one stable, one unstable) collide and annihilate. Afterwards nothing is stable.
Transcriticalẋ = rx − x²Two fixed points pass through each other and exchange stability.
Pitchforkẋ = rx − x³One stable point becomes unstable and gives birth to two stable points at ±√r. A symmetric system picks a side.

The saddle-node has a vivid real-world version, a fish population with logistic growth and constant harvesting h:

ẋ = x(1 − x) − h

Fixed points solve x² − x + h = 0, which has real roots only while h ≤ 1/4. Fish slightly less than that and the population survives at a healthy level. Fish slightly more and the stable state disappears: the population collapses to zero, and turning the quota back down a little doesn't save it once it has fallen below the unstable point. That is a tipping point.

Chapter 3

Two dimensions: linear systems and the phase plane

With two state variables, say position and velocity, the state is a point in the phase plane and f is an arrow at every point. A trajectory follows the arrows. The simplest case is linear:

ẋ = ax + by,   ẏ = cx + dy   ⟺   ẋ = Ax

Solutions are combinations of eλt, where the λ are the eigenvalues of the matrix A. Two numbers decide everything, the trace τ = a + d and the determinant Δ = ad − bc:

λ1,2 = ( τ ± √(τ² − 4Δ) ) / 2
ConditionEigenvaluesPicture
Δ < 0real, opposite signsSaddle: attracts along one direction, repels along the other. Always unstable.
Δ > 0, τ² > 4Δreal, same signNode: straight-ish approach (stable if τ < 0) or escape.
Δ > 0, τ² < 4Δcomplex pairSpiral: rotation at rate Im λ, growth or decay at rate Re λ = τ/2.
Δ > 0, τ = 0purely imaginaryCenter: closed orbits forever, like a frictionless spring ẍ = −x.

The real part of an eigenvalue is again a rate of exponential separation, just like f′(x*) in chapter 1. A saddle has one positive rate: that is local sensitivity to initial conditions, but around a single point only.

Chapter 4

Nonlinear 2D: zoom in until it looks linear

A nonlinear system ẋ = f(x, y), ẏ = g(x, y) can have many fixed points. Near each one, the system looks like the linear system whose matrix is the Jacobian, the matrix of partial derivatives:

J = [ ∂f/∂x   ∂f/∂y ;  ∂g/∂x   ∂g/∂y ] evaluated at (x*, y*)

Classify J with chapter 3's table and you know the local picture. The Hartman–Grobman theorem guarantees the match when no eigenvalue has zero real part. Centers are the delicate exception: a tiny nonlinear term can turn them into slow spirals.

Example: the pendulum

With angle θ and angular velocity ω, a frictionless pendulum obeys θ̇ = ω, ω̇ = −sin θ.

  • Hanging down, (0, 0): J = [0 1; −1 0], so τ = 0, Δ = 1, a center. Small swings go on forever.
  • Balanced upside down, (π, 0): J = [0 1; 1 0], so Δ = −1, a saddle. The slightest nudge grows like et.

The center at the bottom survives the nonlinearity because the energy E = ω²/2 − cos θ is conserved: every trajectory stays on one contour of E, and those contours are closed loops. Swings with enough energy go over the top and rotate instead. The trajectory dividing the two behaviours, the separatrix, runs from saddle to saddle.

Conservative and dissipative systems

How a blob of initial conditions changes in area tells you a lot. Its area changes at a rate equal to the divergence ∂f/∂x + ∂g/∂y.

  • Conservative (divergence 0): areas are preserved, the content of Liouville's theorem. Examples are the frictionless pendulum and Lotka–Volterra predator–prey, which in its scaled form ẋ = x − xy, ẏ = xy − y conserves E = x − ln x + y − ln y. There are no attractors, only orbits that keep their shape.
  • Dissipative (divergence < 0): areas shrink, as with a pendulum with friction. Everything piles up onto an attractor, a set of lower dimension than the phase space.

Chaos comes in both flavours: dissipative chaos lives on a strange attractor (Lorenz, chapter 7), and conservative chaos fills regions of phase space (the double pendulum and three bodies, chapter 10).

Chapter 5

Limit cycles, the Hopf bifurcation, and why 2D can't be chaotic

A limit cycle is an isolated closed orbit that nearby trajectories spiral onto. It is a self-sustained oscillation whose amplitude doesn't depend on how it started: a heartbeat, a squealing brake, the van der Pol circuit ẍ − μ(1 − x²)ẋ + x = 0. Small motions get pumped up (negative damping) and large ones get damped, so the system settles on one in-between loop.

Hopf bifurcation

Consider ẍ + (x² − μ)ẋ + x = 0. At the origin J = [0 1; −1 μ], so τ = μ and Δ = 1: a spiral whose stability flips when μ crosses 0. For small μ > 0, averaging shows the amplitude obeys ṙ = (r/2)(μ − r²/4), so trajectories settle on a cycle of radius r = 2√μ. A fixed point losing stability to a growing oscillation is a Hopf bifurcation. When a cycle appears smoothly like this it is supercritical. In the subcritical case the system jumps to some distant state instead, which is the version that occurs in Lorenz.

Poincaré–Bendixson: the 2D ceiling

In the plane, trajectories can never cross: if they did, the crossing point would have two different futures, contradicting uniqueness. A curve that can't cross itself in a plane is very restricted. The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem makes this precise. A trajectory trapped in a bounded region eventually approaches either

  • a fixed point,
  • a closed orbit (limit cycle), or
  • a loop of fixed points joined by trajectories.

Nothing else. There is no room for a trajectory that wanders forever without repeating. Chaos is impossible in a two-dimensional autonomous flow. To get it you need a third dimension, so that trajectories can pass over and under each other, or you need to step outside continuous flows altogether. Chapter 6 does the second, chapter 7 the first.

Chapter 6

Maps: chaos in one line

A map advances the state in discrete jumps, xn+1 = g(xn): once per generation, per orbit, per camera frame. Because a map jumps instead of flowing, the Poincaré–Bendixson argument doesn't apply, and even a 1D map can be chaotic. That makes maps the cleanest laboratory for chaos.

A fixed point x* = g(x*) is stable when |g′(x*)| < 1, because each step multiplies a small error by g′(x*). Compare f′(x*) < 0 for flows.

The shift map: chaos you can see in decimals

Take xn+1 = 10 xn mod 1, which shifts the decimal point one place right and drops the integer part. Starting from 0.31415926…, the iterates are 0.1415926…, 0.415926…, 0.15926…. The rule is completely deterministic, yet the value after n steps is decided by the n-th digit of the starting value. Know x₀ to 8 digits and you can predict 8 steps, no more. Every step multiplies errors by 10, so its Lyapunov exponent is λ = ln 10 per step.

The logistic map and period doubling

xn+1 = r xn(1 − xn)

A population model with growth rate r. As r increases:

  • 1 < r < 3: a stable fixed point x* = 1 − 1/r, since g′(x*) = 2 − r is inside (−1, 1).
  • r = 3: g′(x*) = −1 and the fixed point gives way to a 2-cycle, which alternates high, low, high, low.
  • r = 1 + √6 ≈ 3.449: period 4, then 8, 16, and so on, with each doubling arriving sooner than the last. The ratio of successive gaps tends to Feigenbaum's constant δ ≈ 4.669, which is the same for every smooth one-hump map.
  • r ≈ 3.5699: infinitely many doublings have happened and the orbit never repeats. This is chaos, interrupted by windows of order (the period-3 window opens at 1 + √8 ≈ 3.828).
  • r = 4: fully chaotic, with λ = ln 2, so errors double every step on average.

The ingredients are visible right in the formula. The map stretches (its slope exceeds 1 over much of the interval) and folds (the hump sends both ends of the interval to the same place, so the orbit stays bounded). Stretching makes neighbours separate, and folding keeps the orbit bounded so the stretching can go on forever. That stretch-and-fold pairing is the mechanism behind every chaotic system on this page.

Chapter 7

The Lorenz attractor

In 1963 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz cut a model of convection, a fluid layer heated from below, down to three variables:

ẋ = σ(y − x),   ẏ = x(r − z) − y,   ż = xy − bz
  • x is the speed of the convection rolls (its sign is the rotation direction), y is the temperature difference between rising and sinking fluid, and z is how far the vertical temperature profile is from a straight line.
  • σ = 10 is the Prandtl number (viscosity against heat diffusion) and b = 8/3 is a geometry factor. r is the heating, measured relative to the heating at which convection starts.
  • The only nonlinearities are the products xz and xy. That is enough.

Turning up the heat

Heating rWhat the system does
r < 1The origin, meaning no convection, is globally stable. Heat simply conducts.
r = 1Pitchfork: the origin becomes a saddle and two fixed points C± = (±√(b(r−1)), ±√(b(r−1)), r−1) appear. They are steady rolls turning one way or the other.
1 < r < 24.74Trajectories spiral into C+ or C−. Above about 13.9 the route there can involve long chaotic-looking transients.
rH = σ(σ+b+3)/(σ−b−1) ≈ 24.74Subcritical Hopf: C± become unstable. Now every fixed point is unstable.
r = 28Lorenz's choice. Motion settles onto the butterfly-shaped strange attractor.

Why a strange attractor has to exist at r = 28

Three facts combine:

  • Bounded. There is a large ellipsoid that trajectories enter and never leave, so nothing escapes to infinity.
  • Dissipative. The divergence is −(σ + 1 + b) ≈ −13.67 everywhere, so every blob of initial conditions shrinks in volume by a factor e−13.67 per time unit. The attractor must therefore have zero volume.
  • No stable resting place. All three fixed points are unstable, and no stable cycle exists at r = 28.

So trajectories are squeezed onto a set of zero volume, can never settle, and can never repeat. The result is a surface-like object of fractal dimension about 2.06. It looks like two sheets but is infinitely many, layered like puff pastry. A trajectory circles one lobe (rolls turning one way), then jumps unpredictably to the other. Nearby trajectories are stretched apart within the sheets and folded back as they wrap from one lobe to the other. That is the same stretch and fold as the logistic map, carried out in a continuous flow with three dimensions of room.

Chapter 8

The Lyapunov exponent λ

Chapter 1 showed that near a fixed point a small error behaves like ef′(x*) t. On a chaotic attractor there is no fixed point to sit near, but the same question still makes sense: follow two trajectories that start a distance δ₀ apart and measure how fast the gap grows. Averaged over a long time, it grows exponentially,

|δ(t)| ≈ |δ₀| eλt,    λ = limt→∞ (1/t) ln( |δ(t)| / |δ₀| )(with δ₀ → 0)

The number λ is the maximal Lyapunov exponent. It is the long-run average stretching rate along a trajectory: chapter 1's f′(x*), averaged over a path that never stops moving.

  • λ < 0: errors die out and the system forgets its initial condition. It is settling to a stable fixed point.
  • λ = 0: errors grow at most polynomially. This is typical of cycles and quasi-periodic motion: two points on the same orbit stay the same distance apart, just offset in time.
  • λ > 0 on a bounded set: errors grow exponentially but can never exceed the size of the attractor. This is the signature of chaos.

The whole spectrum

In n dimensions a tiny sphere of initial conditions is stretched into an ellipsoid whose axes grow like eλ₁t, eλ₂t, …. These n rates form the Lyapunov spectrum, and their sum equals the average divergence from chapter 4, the rate at which volume shrinks. For Lorenz at the standard parameters:

ExponentValueMeaning
λ₁+0.906Stretching within the attractor's sheets. This is the λ for Lorenz.
λ₂0Along the flow: a point slightly ahead on the same trajectory stays slightly ahead.
λ₃−14.57Strong squeezing onto the attractor.
sum−13.67Equals −(σ + 1 + b), the volume contraction rate, as it must.

Reading λ = 0.906 for Lorenz

The units are inverse Lorenz time units, the model's own clock rather than seconds. On average an error

  • grows by a factor e ≈ 2.72 every 1/0.906 ≈ 1.10 time units,
  • doubles every ln 2/0.906 ≈ 0.77 time units, and
  • grows tenfold every ln 10/0.906 ≈ 2.54 time units.

A simulation with step Δt = 0.01 therefore loses a factor e0.00906 of accuracy per step, about 0.9%, and that compounds. The rate is an average: stretching is uneven, strongest as a trajectory swings past the origin and picks a lobe, and mild elsewhere. Measured over a short window, the finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) scatters widely around 0.906.

How it is measured

The Benettin method, used in chaos_dyn/lyapunov.py:

  1. Integrate a reference trajectory and a neighbour δ₀ = 10−8 away.
  2. After each short interval τ, record ln(|δ|/δ₀), then pull the neighbour back to distance δ₀ along the current separation direction.
  3. λ is the sum of the logs divided by the total time.

Rescaling keeps the pair in the small-error regime where growth is exponential. Keeping the direction lets the separation turn toward the most unstable direction on its own. The project's test checks that this gives λ between 0.85 and 0.95 for Lorenz.

Chapter 9

Lyapunov time and the prediction horizon

The Lyapunov time is the reciprocal of the exponent, Tλ = 1/λ: the time for an error to grow by a factor of e. It is the natural clock of a chaotic system, because it measures time in units of "how long until we know e times less". In the chaos-predictability project:

SystemλLyapunov timeIn simulation steps
Lorenz, r = 280.9061.10 time units110 steps at Δt = 0.01
Three-body, Lagrange-triangle family (median FTLE)0.521.9 time unitsabout 95 steps at Δt = 0.02

The horizon formula

Suppose you know the initial state to within δ₀, and a prediction is useless once its error passes some tolerance. Solving δ₀ eλt = tol for t gives the predictability horizon

tp = Tλ · ln( tol / δ₀ )

The logarithm is the whole lesson. With a 10% tolerance:

Initial error δ₀Horizon in Lyapunov timesLorenz, time units

10−16 (double precision)34.538.1

Each factor of 10 in initial accuracy buys only ln 10 ≈ 2.3 more Lyapunov times. Ten thousand times better measurements add the same fixed amount of forecast however good you already are. This is why weather forecasts top out around two weeks: the atmosphere is deterministic, but its Lyapunov time is short, and better instruments push the horizon out only logarithmically. Deterministic does not mean predictable.

Errors made every step

A neural network that predicts one step at a time doesn't just start with an error. It adds a fresh error ε at every step, and each one then grows at rate λ. Summing a geometric series of these errors gives an equivalent one-time initial error

δeff = ε / (eλΔt − 1) ≈ ε / (λΔt)

For Lorenz, λΔt = 0.00906, so a one-step error acts like an initial error about 110 times larger. In practice the measured effect was about 15 times, because not every step's error points in the most unstable direction. That is why the "ε every step" estimate is a conservative one.

On three bodies it loses about 10, and the culprit is close encounters, where gravity's 1/r² pull makes a single step about 30 times harder to learn.

Chapter 10

Pendulums and planets: chaos without friction

Lorenz is dissipative. The two mechanical classics are conservative: energy is fixed, nothing shrinks, and there is no attractor. Chaos then shows up as a region of phase space that a trajectory wanders through, instead of a thin set it settles onto. The Chaos and Predictability page has live twin simulations of both.

The double pendulum

  • The state is two angles and two angular velocities, so the phase space is 4D. Fixed energy confines motion to a 3D surface, which is enough room for chaos.
  • Low energy: the arms barely swing, the equations are nearly linear, and the motion is a blend of two normal modes. It is regular, with λ = 0.
  • High energy: the arms can flip over the top, and which way and when becomes exquisitely sensitive to the start. λ > 0.
  • In between: islands of regular motion sit in a chaotic sea. KAM theory describes how the regular "tori" break up one by one as the energy rises.

The three-body problem

  • Three masses pulling on each other with gravity. In a plane each body has a position and a velocity, so the state has 12 numbers.
  • Energy, momentum and angular momentum are conserved, but that removes only a few dimensions. Poincaré showed in the 1890s that there are not enough conserved quantities to solve the problem in general, and in the process discovered sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
  • Special exact solutions exist. Lagrange's equilateral triangle rotates rigidly but is unstable for equal masses. The figure-eight, found numerically by Moore and proved by Chenciner and Montgomery in 2000, is stable.
  • Close encounters are the hard part. The force grows like 1/r², so a near miss stretches errors violently in a very short time. Two bodies passing close is where both numerical integrators and the learned models struggle.
Chapter 11

When does a system become chaotic?

A working definition, following Strogatz: chaos is aperiodic long-term behaviour in a deterministic system that shows sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Each word matters. "Aperiodic" rules out settling onto a fixed point, cycle or torus. "Deterministic" means no randomness is needed. "Sensitive dependence" means λ > 0.

Necessary conditions

  1. Nonlinearity. A linear system's exponents are just the real parts of its eigenvalues, the same everywhere. It either decays, oscillates or blows up, with no folding.
  2. Enough dimensions.
    • Autonomous flow: at least 3 (Poincaré–Bendixson, chapter 5).
    • A 2D flow with periodic forcing also counts, because time adds a dimension. The forced damped pendulum and the Duffing oscillator are examples.
    • Invertible maps: at least 2 (the Hénon map).
    • Non-invertible maps: 1 is enough (the logistic map).
  3. Stretching. Something unstable, such as a saddle or an unstable spiral, must pull neighbouring states apart, so that λ > 0.
  4. Folding. Something must keep motion bounded and bring it back: a trapping region, conserved energy, the logistic map's hump. Stretching alone is just blow-up. ẋ = x separates neighbours exponentially, but it is not chaotic.
  5. Nowhere stable to settle. If a stable fixed point or cycle exists, many trajectories end there. Lorenz between r ≈ 24.06 and 24.74 has a chaotic attractor alongside stable fixed points, and the initial condition decides which one you get.

None of these is sufficient alone. Lorenz at r = 10 is nonlinear, three-dimensional and bounded, and still boring.

Routes into chaos

Systems usually become chaotic gradually as a parameter is turned. There are a few standard scenarios:

RouteWhat you seeExample
Period doublingPeriods 1, 2, 4, 8, … with gaps shrinking by δ ≈ 4.669Logistic map; dripping faucet; Lorenz at large r
IntermittencyLong nearly periodic stretches broken by chaotic bursts, near a saddle-nodeLogistic map just below the period-3 window
Quasi-periodicityOne frequency, then two, then the torus breaks upConvection experiments (Ruelle–Takens)
Global bifurcationAn orbit connects to a saddle, and chaos appears abruptlyLorenz homoclinic explosion at r ≈ 13.93

A practical checklist for a system you meet

  1. Find the fixed points and linearize. Are any stable?
  2. Check the dimension and whether trajectories stay bounded.
  3. Simulate twins a tiny distance apart. Does the log of their gap rise in a straight line before saturating?
  4. Estimate λ with the Benettin method. If it is positive on a bounded, non-repeating orbit, the system is chaotic, and 1/λ tells you how long your predictions will last.
The one-line summary. Fixed points and their slopes tell you where a system goes. The Lyapunov exponent tells you how fast you lose track of it. A chaotic system is deterministic, yet each Lyapunov time multiplies your uncertainty by e, so its future can be computed only to a horizon that grows with the logarithm of your precision.
